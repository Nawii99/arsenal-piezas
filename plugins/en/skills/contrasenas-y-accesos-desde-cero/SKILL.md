# Passwords and access from scratch

## What it does
It turns the chaos of repeated passwords jotted down anywhere into a simple system: one manager, one master password, two-factor on what matters, and a clear list of who gets into what. It goes slowly, one account at a time.

## What to ask first
1. Where are the passwords today? (head, notebook, browser, phone notes, a chat)
2. How many people share accounts and which ones?
3. Which accounts are critical? Main email, bank, website domain, social networks, accounting/tax advisor.
4. Has anyone left the team without having their access removed?
5. Android phone or iPhone? Windows or Mac computer? The steps need to be adjusted.

## Step by step
1. Start with the main email. It is the key to everything else: whoever gets in there can recover the rest of the accounts.
2. Choose a manager. Bitwarden has a free plan that is enough for one person; Proton Pass and 1Password are paid alternatives. State the price out loud before deciding.
3. Create the master password with three or four words that have nothing to do with each other. Long and memorable, not short and strange.
4. Write the master password and the recovery code on paper. Two copies, in two different physical places. No photos and no files.
5. Turn on two-step verification on the email, the bank, and the domain. Use a code app or a physical key; SMS only if there is no other option.
6. Save each account's backup codes in the manager, not on the same phone that generates the codes.
7. Change passwords in order of importance, not all at once. Five a week is a pace people can sustain.
8. Shared accounts: create a shared folder in the manager instead of passing the password around in chat. If the tool supports separate users, that is better than sharing one account.
9. Make the access list: account, who gets in, for what, and a review every three months.

## What to return
- The access list in a table, with the critical accounts marked.
- A simple calendar of changes by week.
- The three rules the person has to remember without looking anything up.

## What NOT to do
- Never ask them to tell you or type out a password, not even to help. You guide, the person types.
- Do not store or copy a client's login details on your own computer.
- Do not change all the passwords on the same day: if something fails, the person is locked out of their business.
- Do not turn off two-factor to make a process more convenient.

## When a person with professional responsibility is needed
If the email account or the bank has already been used by someone else, this stops being tidying up and becomes an incident: the bank, a police report, and a technician come in. If access has to be removed from someone leaving the company on bad terms, the responsible person should coordinate it with labor and advisory staff, not with the haste of the moment.
